Chapter 3: Probability

Author

Junlong Feng

Econometric estimators are functions of random samples. Probability gives us the language for describing their behavior before the sample is observed. The optional sections at the end correspond to the printed appendix.

1 Random variables and distributions

A random variable is a numerical function of an underlying random outcome. The set of values that it can take with positive probability, or approach arbitrarily closely, is its support. The support is not the same object as the underlying sample space of outcomes.

A variable is discrete when its distribution is concentrated on a finite or countable set. Its probability mass function is

\[ p_X(x)=\Pr(X=x). \]

A Bernoulli variable \(X\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(p)\) has support \(\{0,1\}\) and \(\Pr(X=1)=p\).

The cumulative distribution function (c.d.f.) is defined for every random variable:

\[ F_X(x)=\Pr(X\leq x). \]

If \(X\) has a density \(f_X\), then

\[ F_X(x)=\int_{-\infty}^x f_X(s)\,ds, \qquad \Pr(a<X\leq b)=\int_a^b f_X(s)\,ds. \]

Such a variable is absolutely continuous, and \(\Pr(X=x)=0\) for every \(x\). Probability is not generally the geometric length of an interval: it is the area under the density. Length gives probability only for a suitably normalized uniform distribution.

A normal variable \(X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)\) has density

\[ f_X(x)=\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\left\{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right\}. \]

Normal distributions are important for large-sample approximations; we will not assume that economic variables themselves are normally distributed.

1.1 Joint, marginal, and conditional distributions

The joint distribution of \((X,Y)\) describes their behavior together. Its marginal distributions describe \(X\) and \(Y\) separately. Conditional distributions describe one variable within a subpopulation indexed by the other.

When a joint density exists,

\[ f_X(x)=\int f_{X,Y}(x,y)\,dy, \qquad f_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x)=\frac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{f_X(x)} \]

for \(x\) with \(f_X(x)>0\). Thus

\[ f_{X,Y}(x,y)=f_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x)f_X(x) =f_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y)f_Y(y). \]

The joint distribution contains the marginals, but the marginals alone usually do not reveal how \(X\) and \(Y\) move together.

Independence. \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent, written \(X\perp Y\), if

\[ F_{X,Y}(x,y)=F_X(x)F_Y(y) \]

for every \((x,y)\). When densities exist, this is equivalent to density factorization almost everywhere. If \(X\perp Y\), then \(g(X)\perp h(Y)\) for measurable \(g\) and \(h\).

2 Expectation, variance, covariance, and correlation

For a continuous variable,

\[ \mathbb E(X)=\int x f_X(x)\,dx=\mu_X. \]

For a discrete variable, replace the integral by a sum. Expectation is linear whether or not variables are independent:

\[ \mathbb E(aX+bY)=a\mathbb E(X)+b\mathbb E(Y). \]

Variance and covariance are

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}(X) &=\mathbb E[(X-\mu_X)^2] =\mathbb E(X^2)-\mu_X^2,\\ \operatorname{Cov}(X,Y) &=\mathbb E[(X-\mu_X)(Y-\mu_Y)] =\mathbb E(XY)-\mu_X\mu_Y. \end{aligned} \]

Useful rules include

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}(aX+bY) &=a^2\operatorname{Var}(X)+b^2\operatorname{Var}(Y) +2ab\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y),\\ \operatorname{Cov}(aX+bY,Z) &=a\operatorname{Cov}(X,Z)+b\operatorname{Cov}(Y,Z). \end{aligned} \]

Correlation is

\[ \rho_{XY}=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)} {\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)\operatorname{Var}(Y)}}\in[-1,1] \]

when both variances are positive. Zero correlation need not imply independence.

For a random vector \(X\), the covariance matrix is

\[ \Sigma_X=\mathbb E[(X-\mu_X)(X-\mu_X)']. \]

It is symmetric and positive semidefinite because

\[ a'\Sigma_Xa=\operatorname{Var}(a'X)\geq0. \]

3 Conditional expectation

For a continuous pair,

\[ \mathbb E(Y\mid X=x)=\int y f_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x)\,dy. \]

For fixed \(x\), this is a number. By contrast, \(\mathbb E(Y\mid X)\) is a random variable: it is a function of the still-random \(X\).

Conditional expectation is linear, and functions of the conditioning variable can be taken outside:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb E(aY+bZ\mid X) &=a\mathbb E(Y\mid X)+b\mathbb E(Z\mid X),\\ \mathbb E[g(X)Y\mid X] &=g(X)\mathbb E(Y\mid X). \end{aligned} \]

The law of iterated expectations says

\[ \mathbb E\{\mathbb E(Y\mid X)\}=\mathbb E(Y). \]

If the information in \(Z\) is contained in that in \(X\), then

\[ \mathbb E\{\mathbb E(Y\mid X)\mid Z\}=\mathbb E(Y\mid Z). \]

In particular, \(\mathbb E(X\mid X)=X\).

We say \(Y\) is mean independent of \(X\) if \(\mathbb E(Y\mid X)=\mathbb E(Y)\) almost surely. Full independence implies mean independence in both directions, and mean independence implies zero covariance when the moments exist:

\[ X\perp Y \Longrightarrow \mathbb E(Y\mid X)=\mathbb E(Y) \Longrightarrow \operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=0. \]

4 Random samples and large-sample theory

For cross-sectional applications, we often assume \(W_i=(Y_i,X_i')'\) is i.i.d. across units. This does not require the components within \(W_i\) to be independent. In panel data, repeated observations on the same unit are normally dependent; i.i.d. sampling may instead be imposed across units.

4.1 Convergence in probability and the WLLN

\(W_n\) converges in probability to \(w\), written \(W_n\to_p w\), if, for every \(\varepsilon>0\),

\[ \Pr(\lVert W_n-w\rVert>\varepsilon)\to0. \]

When \(W_n\) is an estimator, this is consistency.

Weak law of large numbers. If \(X_1,\ldots,X_n\) are i.i.d. random vectors with \(\mathbb E\lVert X_i\rVert<\infty\), then

\[ \bar X_n=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nX_i\to_p\mathbb E(X_i). \]

Under finite variance, the scalar intuition is \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar X_n)=\operatorname{Var}(X_i)/n\to0\).

4.2 Convergence in distribution and the CLT

\(W_n\) converges in distribution to \(W\), written \(W_n\to_d W\), if \(\mathbb E[g(W_n)]\to\mathbb E[g(W)]\) for every bounded continuous function \(g\). For scalar variables, this is equivalent to c.d.f. convergence at every continuity point of the limiting c.d.f. It is not correct to require convergence of probabilities for every possible set.

Multivariate central limit theorem. If \(X_i\) are i.i.d. \(k\times1\) random vectors with mean \(\mu\) and finite covariance matrix \(\Sigma\), then

\[ \sqrt n(\bar X_n-\mu)\to_d N(0,\Sigma). \]

The limit is standard normal only after appropriate standardization. \(\Sigma\) includes cross-component covariances as well as marginal variances.

4.3 Continuous mapping and Slutsky

For a continuous function \(g\), the continuous mapping theorem transfers convergence through \(g\). Slutsky’s theorem says that if \(X_n\to_dX\) and \(Y_n\to_p c\) for a constant \(c\), then

\[ X_n+Y_n\to_dX+c,\qquad X_nY_n\to_dcX,\qquad X_n/Y_n\to_dX/c\quad(c\neq0). \]

5 Optional: probability spaces and sigma-algebras

A probability space is a triple \((\mathcal S,\mathcal F,P)\):

  • \(\mathcal S\) is the sample space of elementary outcomes;
  • \(\mathcal F\) is a sigma-algebra of events;
  • \(P:\mathcal F\to[0,1]\) assigns probability one to \(\mathcal S\) and is countably additive over disjoint events.

A random variable is a measurable map \(X:\mathcal S\to\mathbb R\). Its distribution is the measure induced on the real line. This separates the underlying outcome space from the support of the numerical variable.

6 Optional: continuous versus absolutely continuous

A variable is continuous (atomless) if \(\Pr(X=x)=0\) for every \(x\), equivalently if its c.d.f. is continuous. It is absolutely continuous if its c.d.f. is an integral of a density. Every absolutely continuous distribution is continuous, but the converse is false. The Cantor distribution has a continuous c.d.f. but no density with respect to ordinary length.

7 Optional: conditioning on a zero-probability event

When \(\Pr(X=x)=0\), an ordinary ratio of probabilities cannot define \(\Pr(Y\leq y\mid X=x)\). A regular conditional distribution supplies a valid version. When a joint density exists and \(f_X(x)>0\),

\[ f_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x)=\frac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{f_X(x)}. \]

Limits over shrinking intervals are useful intuition but require regularity conditions and are not the general definition.

8 Optional: a bivariate normal demonstration

A pair \((X,Y)\) is jointly normal if every linear combination \(aX+bY\) is normal. For a jointly normal pair, zero covariance is equivalent to independence. This is special to the joint-normal family.

9 Optional: WLLN and CLT simulation

For Bernoulli\((0.5)\) observations, the WLLN predicts concentration around \(0.5\), while the CLT predicts

\[ \frac{\sqrt n(\bar X_n-0.5)}{0.5}\to_dN(0,1). \]

10 Optional: delta method

Suppose \(X_n\in\mathbb R^k\) satisfies

\[ \sqrt n(X_n-\theta)\to_dN(0,\Sigma), \]

and \(g:\mathbb R^k\to\mathbb R^\ell\) is continuously differentiable near \(\theta\). Let \(G(\theta)\) be the \(\ell\times k\) Jacobian. Then

\[ \sqrt n\{g(X_n)-g(\theta)\} \to_dN\left(0,G(\theta)\Sigma G(\theta)'\right). \]

For scalar \(g\), \(G(\theta)\) is a row vector. If the first derivative is zero, the first-order result is degenerate and a higher-order delta method may be needed.
